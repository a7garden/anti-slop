Design examples

These panels are illustrative designs for teaching — not actual product results, screenshots, or measured outcomes. Product names, dates, and numbers are invented for this sheet. The paired examples preserve supported capabilities and task records while changing their wording and arrangement. These are teaching alternatives, not controlled usability experiments.

Pair A — week planner: incidental default vs. deliberate arrangement

Both panels show the same illustrative product, “Weekdesk,” a local week planner that supports tasks, due dates, and completion — nothing more. The left panel is a generic marketing arrangement of those facts; the right panel is one deliberately designed, restrained arrangement that puts the week itself first.

  • Arrangement 1 leads with feature descriptions; the same four task records, due dates, and completion states sit below them in a smaller, quieter list. No usability measurement is claimed: it is one generic arrangement, not a proven failure.
  • Arrangement 2 leads with the week’s real content, gives one accent color two jobs (primary action, due-today state), and limits the type scale to a few sizes. It is one coherent choice, not the only valid one.
  • Neither panel adds features: both show exactly tasks, due dates, and completion.

Pair B — navigation: inconsistent boxes vs. consistent system

The same four labels and the same four meanings appear in both panels. Only the craft of the icon boxes, strokes, and label edges differs.

  • Variant 1 mixes circle, square, pill, and dashed boxes at different sizes and stroke weights; labels start at different left edges with four type treatments. Nothing is broken — but the drift reads as several systems.
  • Variant 2 gives every item the same box size, corner radius, stroke, and glyph area, and one label rule on one baseline. The labels, not the boxes, carry the meaning.
  • Consistency here is craft, not decoration: it costs no color and no extra elements.

Example C — purposeful identity, retained unchanged

A supplied festival treatment with a violet gradient and serif display type, shown once, exactly as supplied. There is no “after” panel because no redesign is warranted.

  • Retain this treatment. The gradient and serif type serve the brief’s identity; the schedule and ticket action have distinct, visible places within the composition.
  • A bright palette, a gradient, and a serif headline are not defects to neutralize. Replacing this identity with a neutral default would erase a sound choice, not fix one.
  • This panel illustrates a judgment call on arrangement, not a legibility measurement; a real product would still verify contrast, states, and behavior at their actual values.
